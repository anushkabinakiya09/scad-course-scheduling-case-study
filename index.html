01Context and challenge

A schedule arrives before students can judge it.

First-years don't pick their own classes. They either accept a schedule they can't evaluate or change it without knowing the effect, which can slow degree progress or overload a quarter.

Research question

How do first-year students decide whether an assigned schedule fits their needs, and whose advice do they trust?

03Research approach

The survey showed how common an experience was. Interviews explained why.

Step 1 · Measure

Screened survey

101 undergraduates. Two screener questions kept only students who had looked into their classes, professors or schedule.

Step 2 · Understand

Semi-structured interviews

12 sessions, each anchored to one real scheduling decision, with neutral follow-up questions.

Step 3 · Cluster

Affinity mapping

Interview observations grouped into 11 themes, then narrowed to 6 insights and 4 takeaways.

Step 4 · Sequence

Journey mapping

Themes and survey counts placed on a five-stage timeline per cohort, to show when each problem happens.

6 insights

  1. Insight 1Having a schedule assigned can be reassuring while leaving students unsure which decisions still belong to them.
  2. Insight 2Students may recognize what they need from a schedule only after their options have narrowed.
  3. Insight 3Course sequencing can shape students’ access to skills and opportunities before graduation.
  4. Insight 4Students develop scheduling preferences by learning which trade-offs work in their daily lives.
  5. Insight 5Students’ confidence in a resource depends on the question and how well their circumstances are understood.
  6. Insight 6Losing access to one course can disrupt the balance of an entire quarter.

4 takeaways

  1. 1Assigned schedules reassure, but leave choice unclear.
  2. 2Guidance must be timely and fit the decision.
  3. 3Workable schedules depend on the whole day.
  4. 4Course access shapes learning and opportunity.

04Key findings

Three findings, each backed by a number and a voice.

Finding 1

Schedule set. Choices unclear.

Assigned schedules ease registration but leave students unsure of their options, how to change them, and when to act. One student learned a change was possible in week seven or eight, after the window had closed.

37 of 101

survey respondents wished they had known when and how to request a schedule change, including 21 of 50 first-years

3 of 6

first-years interviewed changed their fall schedule

What students need

Clarity about their choices and how to act on them before options narrow.

See the recommendation →

Finding 2

Different questions. Different sources.

Students bring different concerns and piece guidance together from several sources. Official information is scattered across MySCAD, the website, a pamphlet and Google, so older students become the most helpful source for those who know any.

Which sources did first-years rate most helpful? Ratings out of 5

Older students4.3
Academic advisors4.2
SCAD website2.9

Small sample: 7 to 10 ratings per source. Upperclassmen also rated older students 4.3 (18 ratings).

In the interviews

One interviewee checked the degree planner for prerequisites and asked peers which courses to take first.

Another changed class times with an advisor and asked older students for professor recommendations.

What students need

Reliable, personalized guidance that brings relevant information together and helps them make informed scheduling decisions.

When, why and what it unlocks.

A course can count toward a degree and still matter strategically for internships and portfolios.

An understanding of how course timing and sequence shape future learning and opportunities.

07Recommendations and outcomes

What should change, and the finding behind it.

1
↑ From finding 1

Say on the schedule itself that it can be changed.

Put how, where and by when next to the schedule in MySCAD, so finding out doesn't depend on who a student knows.

2
↑ From finding 2

Make advisors open about the details, and pair first-years with upperclass mentors.

Advisors say plainly why each class was chosen and what can change. Upperclassmen can act as mentors, so the know-how some students get from older friends reaches everyone.

3
↑ From finding 3

Show the degree path with the schedule.

Explain why each class is there, what it unlocks and which courses can move, before students register on their own.

08Impact

The fix is timing and visibility, not new rules.

For students

Students can already change a schedule. They need to be told so while there is still time to act.

For SCAD advising

The maps mark where each breakdown happens, so effort can go to two moments: before welcome week and the first week of class.

For the team

The maps combined both methods into one view per cohort and gave the recommendations their starting questions.
